    In short

    1. What it is

      A way to check a weather hunch against 45 years of NASA data, for 49 UK towns and 26 world cities.

    2. What you can ask

      Heat, warm and muggy nights, when the seasons start and end, frost, rain, dry spells, wind, dry soil, sunshine, and how much heating or cooling homes need.

    3. What it can’t do

      Snow, fog, storms, forecasts, or your exact street. Each place is a square about 50 km across.

    4. How to use it

      Ask in your own words, or pick a place and a hunch. Make a guess, then see the verdict.

      The science

      1. Daily weather

        Every day from 1981 to 2025 for 75 places, about 1.2 million days in all, from NASA POWER (MERRA-2).

      2. One number a year

        Each hunch becomes one number for every year. These bars are London’s heatwave days.

      3. Is there a trend?

        A test checks whether later years beat earlier ones more often than chance would. The size is the typical change every 10 years. (Mann-Kendall test, Hamed-Rao version; Sen’s slope.)

      4. Could it be luck?

        The years are shuffled into a random order 1,000 times. If the shuffles often show a trend as strong, it could be chance.

      5. The verdict

        Together they give one of four answers, shown below.

      Across all 1,142 trends

      • 448Yes, it’s real. The trend is very unlikely to be chance.
      • 496Signs of it, not proven. It points that way, but it could still be luck.
      • 164Not backed by the data. No clear trend either way.
      • 34It’s actually the opposite. A clear trend, the other way round.

      The verdict is about the trend in the data, not a score for your guess.

      The chat box

      • A language model works out which test you mean: Google’s Gemini, or a small model (MiniLM) in your browser when Gemini can’t be reached.
      • Neither one supplies a number. Every figure comes from the NASA results, and a Gemini sentence with a number that isn’t on the card is thrown away.
      • On 75 test questions, both Gemini and the on-device model picked the right test (or rightly said “can’t test that”) every time. Both were tuned on earlier misses from this set, so brand-new sentences may do a little worse.
      • A question the data can’t test gets a general answer from Gemini, marked as not coming from the NASA data.

      What it can’t tell you

      • Each value is an average over a square about 50 km wide, so peaks read low. London’s hottest day shows about 34.5 °C, where stations hit about 40 °C. Trends still hold.
      • “Unusually hot” means hot for that place, so the bar is different in each town.
      • Years with no heatwave or no frost have no date, so they drop out of those trends.
      • Rain jumps around a lot from year to year, so most rain trends only reach “signs of it, not proven”.
      • Humidity, wind and soil come from NASA’s weather model rather than measurements, so those trends are less certain than temperature.
      • Belfast, Aberdeen, Toronto, Lisbon and Cape Town use the square next door, because their own is mostly sea or lake. Singapore’s square is mostly sea with no better one nearby, so its numbers are provisional, and its rain stops in 2020 because the data is faulty after that. Sunshine data starts in 1984. Sydney, Sao Paulo, Johannesburg, Cape Town and Rio de Janeiro count July to June, so they start in 1982.
      • It shows that something changed, not why.
      • Snow, fog and storms aren’t covered.
