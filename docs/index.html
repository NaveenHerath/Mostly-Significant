    How this works

    Daily weather from NASA POWER (MERRA-2), 1981 to 2025, for 12 UK towns and 26 world cities. Each hunch is turned into one yearly number, like heatwave days a year.

    A Mann-Kendall test (Hamed-Rao version) checks whether that number has a real trend, and Sen’s slope gives the change per decade. Then the years are shuffled 1,000 times to see how often luck alone makes a trend this strong.

    What the verdicts mean

    • Yes, it’s real. The trend is very unlikely to be chance.
    • Leaning that way. It points your way, but chance can’t be ruled out yet.
    • Not backed by the data. No clear trend either way.
    • It’s actually the opposite. A clear trend, the other way round.

    The verdict is about the trend in the data, not a score for your guess.

    What it can’t tell you

    • Each value is an average over a square about 50 km wide, so peaks read low. London’s hottest day shows about 34.5 °C, where stations hit about 40 °C. Trends still hold.
    • “Unusually hot” means hot for that place, so the bar is different in each town.
    • Years with no heatwave or no frost have no date, so they drop out of those trends.
    • Rain jumps around a lot from year to year, so most rain trends only reach “leaning”.
    • Belfast, Aberdeen, Toronto, Lisbon and Cape Town use the square next door, because their own is mostly sea or lake. Singapore’s square is mostly sea with no better one nearby, so its numbers are provisional, and its rain stops in 2020 because the data is faulty after that. Sunshine data starts in 1984. Sydney, Sao Paulo, Johannesburg, Cape Town and Rio de Janeiro count July to June, so they start in 1982.
    • It shows that something changed, not why.
    • Snow, fog, wind, storms and humidity aren’t covered.
